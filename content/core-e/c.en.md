## 3.1 Curriculum requirements

Understand the basic ideas of intellectual property and copyright; understand the benefits and risks of different licensing schemes such as freeware, shareware, open source software and copyrighted software from the perspectives of users and software developers; relate acts of possible infringement of copyright in software and Internet piracy, discuss the social, legal and economic implications of infringement, develop the habit of acknowledging the source of information, and use multimedia materials appropriately; be aware of methods that reduce the theft of digital property, such as digital watermarks and digital signatures; understand the legal consequences of copyright infringement in Hong Kong, especially infringement related to education.

## 3.2 Basic concepts of intellectual property

**Intellectual property** is the set of rights the law gives creators and inventors to protect their creations and inventions from copying or misappropriation. Protecting intellectual property rewards creators for their efforts and encourages innovation.

| Type | What it protects | Registration needed? | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Copyright (©) | Original literary, musical, dramatic and artistic works, computer programs, sound recordings, films, etc. | No; it arises automatically when the work is created | Novels, photos, digital drawings, software, songs |
| Patent | Inventions, such as new products or processes | Yes | New medicines, new battery technologies |
| Registered design | The shape and appearance of a product | Yes | The distinctive shape of a phone or a piece of furniture |
| Trade mark (™, ®) | Signs and names identifying a trader's goods and services | Yes (® means registered; ™ marks a sign used as a trade mark) | Company logos, brand names |

- Copyright protects the **expression** of a work, not ideas, facts, data, names, titles or short phrases.
- Copyright exists even if a work shows no © or copyright notice; a copyright notice serves as a reminder to others.

⚠ The creator of a work does not need to register copyright: it arises automatically when the work is created. Do not write "apply for copyright at the Intellectual Property Department"; trade marks, patents and designs are what must be registered.

## 3.3 Software licences and types of software

When buying software, users obtain only the right to use it under the **software licence**, not ownership of the software. A software licence is a legal contract that sets out how the user may use the software.

| Licence | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Single-user licence | Allows use on only one computer at a time |
| Multi-user licence (site licence) | Allows a specified number of users, or multiple computers at a specified location (e.g. a school or office), to use the software |

**Common types of software**

| Type | Price | Source code | Main features | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Commercial software (copyrighted software) | A licence must be bought | Not provided | Technical support provided; used according to licence terms | Office suites, professional image editing software |
| Shareware | Free during the trial period, then must be paid for | Not provided | "Try before you buy"; trial versions may have limited functions or add watermarks | A compression utility with a 30-day trial |
| Freeware | Free, with no time limit | Not provided | Still protected by copyright and cannot be modified; some allow only non-commercial use; limited or no support | PDF readers, web browsers |
| Open source software | Usually free | Open | Allows the source code to be used, modified and redistributed, subject to the licence conditions | Linux, Firefox, LibreOffice |
| Public domain software | Free | Usually open | The author gives up copyright, so anyone may freely use, modify and distribute it | The SQLite database engine |

- Shareware and freeware are both copyrighted software; "free" does not mean that it may be copied, modified or resold at will.
- Open source licences generally allow commercial use, but often attach conditions, e.g. the original author's copyright notice must be kept when redistributing, or modified versions must be released under the same licence.
- Subscription software (paid for monthly or yearly) is extension content.

[adapted from 2026 Mock P1B 4(c)] John modifies an open source program downloaded from Peter's website and sells it as commercial software. As long as he follows the conditions of that open source licence, such as keeping the original author's copyright notice, he does not infringe Peter's copyright.

## 3.4 Benefits and risks of different software

**From the user's perspective**

| Type | Benefits | Risks |
|---|---|---|
| Commercial software | Complete functions, more stable; technical support and updates | Higher licence fees; source code cannot be modified |
| Shareware | Can be tried before deciding to buy | Must be paid for after the trial; trial versions have limited functions |
| Freeware | Free to use | Limited support; cannot be modified; versions from unknown sources may contain malware; may collect user data or include advertisements |
| Open source software | Free; can be modified as needed; developers worldwide collaborate, so bugs are fixed quickly and the software improves continuously | Limited official technical support; some software is harder to install and set up |

**From the software developer's perspective**

| Type | Benefits | Risks |
|---|---|---|
| Commercial software | Income from every licence sold; source code kept secret, so competitors find it hard to copy | Users must pay, so there may be fewer users |
| Shareware | Free trials promote the software and attract users to buy | No income if users do not buy after the trial |
| Freeware | Easily attracts many users and builds a reputation | No direct profit from licence fees |
| Open source software | Attracts a developer community to help improve and test it | No profit from licence fees; the source code is public, so others can develop similar products from it |

> ✔ **(1), (2) and (3)**.
> ✘ Thinking that open source only means "the source code can be seen" and leaving out (3).

[2024 P1A Q38] The correct statements about open source software are that everyone can use and modify the source code, and everyone can rename and redistribute the software. "The software developers are responsible for providing software updates" is not correct. Only 56% of candidates answered correctly.

[2023 P1A Q34] The advantage of open source software over freeware is that users can improve the source code and share it with the community; "it is easier to set up and use" and "it is cross-platform" are not correct.

[2023 P1A Q39] Many companies prefer commercial software to open source software because commercial software developers can provide better support. "Open source software contains more bugs" and "companies cannot modify open source software" are not correct.

## 3.5 Copyright infringement and online piracy

**Plagiarism** is copying someone else's work without permission and presenting it as one's own; copying a copyrighted work is **copyright infringement**. Illegally copied software is called **pirated software**.

**Examples of software copyright infringement**
- violating the licence terms, e.g. installing software with a single-user licence on several computers at the same time;
- copying software bought by a school or company under a site licence for use at home;
- continuing to use shareware after the trial period without paying;
- giving or selling software to someone else while keeping it on your own computer;
- installing or distributing pirated software.

**Online piracy** means downloading or distributing copyrighted films, TV programmes, music, games and software over the Internet without authorisation, through peer-to-peer (P2P) file sharing, pirate websites and cloud file-sharing platforms. Some websites claim that "it is not illegal if deleted within 24 hours" or that "it is only promotion"; these claims have no legal basis. P2P sharing software itself is legal; using it to share copyrighted content is what is illegal.

**Implications of infringement**

| Aspect | Implications |
|---|---|
| Social | Creators do not get their due rewards, so they are less willing to create and society becomes less innovative |
| Economic | Software, film and music industries lose income, affecting jobs; prices of genuine products may rise as a result |
| Legal | Infringers may face civil claims; serious cases are prosecuted and may lead to fines and imprisonment |

> ⚠ **[2025 P1A Q40] Which of the following unethical behaviours relate to intellectual property? (1) Downloading a pirated copy of a movie (2) Sending an email with a virus to a friend (3) Hacking into an online banking system (4) Uploading a video of a TV programme to the Internet**
> ✔ **(1) and (4)** only.
> ✘ Choosing (2) or (3): spreading viruses and hacking are computer crimes or security issues, not intellectual property issues.

> ⚠ **[2024 P1A Q37] Mary is a photographer and shares her photos with Peter and John. Which of the following will probably violate her intellectual property rights? (1) Mary shares the photos with Peter and John via BitTorrent (BT) (2) Peter sells the photos to an advertising company (3) Peter edits the photos using illegally copied software**
> ✔ **(2)** only.
> ✘ Only 40% of candidates answered correctly. In (1), Mary is the copyright owner and may share her own photos; (3) infringes the copyright of the software, not Mary's.

[2012 Practice Paper P1A Q37] Mrs Lee does not allow her son to use BitTorrent freeware on her home computer; the sound reason is that he may use it to download illegal software. The BT software itself is not illegal.

## 3.6 Methods to reduce the theft of digital property

| Method | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Digital watermark | Embeds information about the author or source in images, videos, audio or documents; a visible watermark clearly shows the owner, while a hidden watermark cannot be seen and can be used to trace misuse |
| Digital signature | The author signs with their own **private key**, and others verify with the author's **public key**, proving the origin of the work and confirming that the content has not been altered |
| Copyright notice | E.g. "© 2026 Chan Tai Man. All rights reserved.", reminding others that the work is protected by copyright |
| Encryption or access control | Protecting files with passwords, or requiring a login to view |
| Restricting user actions | Disabling download, save, right-click and screenshot functions; showing only low-resolution versions online |

> ⚠ **[2024 P1B 3(d)(ii)] Suggest two measures to protect the copyright of the past papers online.**
> ✔ Add a watermark or digital signature to the papers; add a copyright notice; encrypt the PDF files with a password or use a login page to limit access; disable the print screen, right-click, download or save functions. (Any two)
> ✘ "Apply for or register copyright" was not accepted, as copyright arises automatically; "acknowledge the source" is not a protective measure. The HKEAA reminded candidates that two answers in the same category (e.g. two kinds of watermark) score only one mark.

[2012 Practice Paper P1A Q35] Digital watermarks can effectively reduce the theft of digital intellectual property; setting up firewalls and updating virus definition files are computer security measures that cannot stop works from being copied.

## 3.7 Legal consequences of copyright infringement in Hong Kong

Copyright in Hong Kong is protected by the **Copyright Ordinance (Cap. 528)**. Computer programs and their manuals are literary works and are likewise protected.

| Organisation | Duties |
|---|---|
| Customs and Excise Department | Enforces intellectual property laws, investigating and prosecuting infringers |
| Intellectual Property Department | Advises on intellectual property policy and legislation; registers trade marks, patents and designs; promotes and educates the public on protecting intellectual property |

**Duration of copyright**: copyright in literary, dramatic, musical and artistic works generally lasts until 50 years after the author's death; for sound recordings, 50 years from when the recording is made, or 50 years from release if it is released within that period.

**Legal consequences**
- **Civil**: the copyright owner may claim damages from the infringer and apply to the court for an injunction to stop further infringement.
- **Criminal**: making, selling, importing or distributing infringing copies for business, or distributing infringing copies to an extent that prejudicially affects the copyright owner, is a criminal offence. The general maximum penalty is a fine of $50,000 for each infringing copy and 4 years' imprisonment; making or possessing an article specifically designed for making infringing copies carries a maximum fine of $500,000 and 8 years' imprisonment.
- In 2005, a Hong Kong man who used BT to upload three copyrighted films was convicted by the court, the world's first criminal case involving BT.

**Use related to education**: the Copyright Ordinance allows works to be used for teaching under specified conditions, e.g. [adapted from 2025 Mock P1A Q39] a teacher playing a clip from a TV programme in class for teaching purposes with no commercial purpose. But this is a conditional exemption: uploading teaching materials to a public website or copying a whole book for students may still infringe copyright.

[2012 Practice Paper P1B 2(e)(ii)] Downloading and installing pirated software may lead to a fine and imprisonment.

## 3.8 Acknowledging sources and academic honesty

When quoting others' words, ideas, photos or videos, acknowledge the source; when using others' works publicly online, first obtain the copyright owner's permission. Quoting a short extract for criticism or research is "fair dealing", but the source must be acknowledged; Hong Kong law does not say that "using less than a certain percentage is allowed".

> ⚠ **[2023 P1B 1(d)(ii)] Mary and Peter want to use images found on the Internet in their competition project. In terms of copyright, what should they do before using the images? Give two suggestions.**
> ✔ Acknowledge the source of the images; get the consent of the copyright owner; pay for the right to use them; use openly licensed images instead (e.g. Creative Commons). (Any two)
> ✘ Thinking that "images found online are free to use".

**School-based assessment and academic honesty**: in its 2025 report, the HKEAA noted that a student's SBA program code was found to be plagiarised; the SBA task was given zero marks and the subject grade was lowered by one level. Copying others' words or ideas, including content generated by AI tools, and presenting them as one's own is strictly prohibited; the use of AI tools must be acknowledged just like other reference sources.

## 3.9 Self-test

<details><summary>1. State what copyright, trade marks and patents each protect. Which need registration?</summary>

Copyright protects original works; trade marks protect brand names and logos; patents protect inventions. Trade marks and patents need registration; copyright arises automatically.
</details>

<details><summary>2. Which of the following is protected by copyright? A. A mathematical formula B. A historical fact C. An original digital drawing D. A person's name</summary>

**C**. Copyright does not protect formulas, facts or names.
</details>

<details><summary>3. The bottom of a website shows "© 2027 MetaFireFly™. All rights reserved." If this notice is removed, what happens to the copyright of the website content?</summary>

Nothing changes. Copyright arises automatically when a work is created; the notice is only a reminder to others.
</details>

<details><summary>4. A secondary school wants to install the same software on 50 computers in its computer room. Which licence should it buy? Give one benefit.</summary>

A multi-user licence (site licence). It is more cost-effective than buying a single-user licence for each computer, and easier to manage.
</details>

<details><summary>5. Give two differences between shareware and freeware.</summary>

Shareware has a trial period after which it must be paid for, while freeware has no time limit; trial versions of shareware may have limited functions or add watermarks, while freeware offers all its functions; paid shareware usually comes with technical support. (Any two)
</details>

<details><summary>6. Give one similarity and one difference between open source software and freeware.</summary>

Similarity: both are usually free to use. Difference: open source software makes its source code public and allows modification and redistribution; freeware does not provide the source code and cannot be modified.
</details>

<details><summary>7. A company is considering switching its computers' operating system from commercial software to open source Linux to save costs. Give two factors to consider first.</summary>

Whether existing software and hardware are compatible; the training staff will need; whether technical support is sufficient (any two).
</details>

<details><summary>8. Nga Ting downloads shareware and keeps using it after the trial period without paying. Is this copyright infringement?</summary>

Yes. Shareware is protected by copyright, and its licence requires payment to continue using it after the trial period.
</details>

<details><summary>9. After paying to upgrade some software, Ka Ho claims he has the right to put it on his personal web page for others to download. Do you agree?</summary>

No. Buying software gives only the right to use it under the licence, not the right to redistribute it.
</details>

<details><summary>10. Explain how a digital watermark protects a photographer's intellectual property. Then give another protective measure.</summary>

A digital watermark embeds information such as the photographer's name and source in the photo, so even if the photo is copied or modified, ownership can still be traced and proved. Another measure: add a copyright notice; show only low-resolution versions online; disable the download function (any one).
</details>

<details><summary>11. Which key is used to create a digital signature, and which to verify it?</summary>

Create: the work owner's private key. Verify: the work owner's public key.
</details>

<details><summary>12. In Hong Kong, which department enforces intellectual property laws? What legal consequences may copyright infringement lead to?</summary>

The Customs and Excise Department. The copyright owner may make civil claims and apply for an injunction; serious cases are criminal offences punishable by fines and imprisonment.
</details>
